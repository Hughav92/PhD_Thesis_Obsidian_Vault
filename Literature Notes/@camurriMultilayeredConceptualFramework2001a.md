>[!Synth]
>%% begin synth %%
>
>**Contribution**:: This paper presents a four layer model for the analysis of expressive gesture in artistic contexts. The model consists of a foundational layer representing the [[Physical Quantity|physical signals]] of the gesture, followed by a second layer representing features and statistical parameters extracted from the physical signal. Above this is a layer representing mid-level features and maps and above this a layer for concepts and structures. An intended aim of the model is to provide a method through which to map high-level concepts across modalities through matching concepts and working top-down to arrive at features and signals. Two examples of the framework's application are provided, both of which employ the video-based EyesWeb motion analysis system.

> [!Abstract]
>
> The paper aims at (i) understanding expressiveness in gestures using computational modeling and (ii) exploit this understanding in artistic applications, where the enhancement of the expressiveness in interactive music/dance/video systems is a major goal. A multi-layered conceptual framework is presented and examples are given of its use in interactive art performances.

> [!Quantity of Motion]
>**Modality**:: Video
>
>**Operationalisation**:: Full body. Silhouette is extracted using EyesWeb software. QOM calculated as sum of difference between frames of extracted silhouette over previous *k* frames.
>
>**Use**:: Used as a motion feature for the first example of multilayer model.
>
>**Other**:: Potentially first use of the term in computational music-related body motion analysis.

<mark style="background-color: #000000">Quote</mark>
> The dancer’s silhouette is extracted using background subtraction and a value proportional to the actual quantity of motion is calculated in the following way (roughly): the difference between the silhouette in the current and previous frame is computed, then the last k differences obtained in this way are summed, thus obtaining an image as a sum of the last k differences between couples of frames. The resulting area of such image is an approximation of the quantity of motion. An adaptive threshold, depending on the running average of the quantity of motion in a window of 1 second is then applied in order to distinguish motion and pause phases. Duration of pause and motion phases is measured and the ratio between such durations in a window of 1second is taken as one of the contributors to the measurement of the fluentness of the movement. Actually fluent movements are also continuous with few and short breaks, while more rigid, strong movements are emphasized by more frequent and, eventually, long pauses. Both quantity of motion and fluentness features are situated at Layer II.

The resulting area of such image is an approximation of the quantity of motion. An adaptive threshold, depending on the running average of the quantity of motion in a window of 1 second is then applied in order to distinguish motion and pause phases. Duration of pause and motion phases is measured and the ratio between such durations in a window of 1second is taken as one of the contributors to the measurement of the fluentness of the movement. Actually fluent movements are also continuous with few and short breaks, while more rigid, strong movements are emphasized by more frequent and, eventually, long pauses. Both quantity of motion and fluentness features are situated at Layer II.
